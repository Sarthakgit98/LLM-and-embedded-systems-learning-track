01Bayes' Theorem

Intuition — evidence should update belief

You hold a belief before seeing evidence (the prior). Evidence arrives. Bayes' theorem is the update rule: it tells you exactly how much to move your belief (to the posterior). It's the only consistent way to fold new information into an old opinion — which is why it's the foundation of spam filters, medical diagnostics, and (later) the Bayesian view of machine learning.

The master move — flip the conditioning

Real life hands you conditional probabilities in the easy direction: "if a person is sick, how often does the test light up?" — that's P(+ | sick), directly measurable. But you want the hard direction: "given a positive test, how likely are you sick?" — P(sick | +). Bayes is the bridge between the two. Same ingredients, different order.

The base-rate trap (why Bayes is shocking): a test that's 99% accurate can still leave you with only a 50/50 chance of being sick. In 10,000 people, 100 are sick (1%) — 99 of them test positive. But of the 9,900 healthy people, 1% — 99 people — also test positive (false alarms). So among all positive tests, only 99 of 198 are real: 50%. The rare disease means false positives from the huge healthy crowd drown out the true positives.

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)   ·   P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|¬A)P(¬A)
The mammogram tree — 10,000 people, 1% sick, test is 99% sensitive with 1% false positives
The highlighted boxes are everyone who tested positive. Bayes asks: what fraction of them are actually sick? The base rate (1% sick) is doing the heavy lifting.

When to use it — and why over alternatives

  • You have the conditionals in the "wrong direction." Medical tests (accuracy of test known, diagnosis unknown), spam filters (P(word|spam) measurable, P(spam|word) wanted), diagnostic reasoning in general.
  • Chosen over plain conditional probability whenever you can't measure the direction you need directly — which is almost always the real-world situation. Plain P(A|B) is a definition; Bayes is the tool for actually computing it.
  • Later payoff: the Bayesian view of machine learning (posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence) is the "other half" of MLE — see sections 02 and 08.

From our discussion

Bayes is the answer to MLE's most common confusion. MLE fixes the data and asks "which θ makes it most likely?" — the frequentist question. Bayes fixes nothing and asks "how plausible is θ now, after seeing the data?" — adding a prior. Same ingredient (the likelihood!), different question. That one sentence dissolves most of the Bayes-vs-MLE confusion you'll meet in ML tutorials.

From our discussion — "what is the numerator, really?"

The cleanest way to see P(A|B): instead of dividing by 1 (the whole world), divide by P(B) — zoom into B's chunk and ask what share of it is A. The numerator P(B|A)·P(A) is then nothing mysterious: by the chain rule it is the joint P(A∩B) — the overlap corner — still measured as a fraction of the whole. Why the product? Because P(B|A) is a fraction of A's chunk ("99% of sick people test +"), and multiplying by P(A) converts it to a fraction of everyone ("99% of the 1% = 0.99% of all people") — the multiplication is the "and". Why write it that way instead of P(A∩B)? Because the overlap is exactly what you can't measure directly — you only know the base rate P(A) and the easy-direction conditional P(B|A), so Bayes builds the corner out of those. The denominator P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|¬A)P(¬A) is the size of the very chunk you zoomed into (198 people: 99 true + 99 false positives). So the formula reads: overlap corner ÷ size of the B-chunk = fraction of B that is A. Bonus symmetry: the same corner has two routes — A→B (P(B|A)P(A)) and B→A (P(A|B)P(B)) — the chain rule says they must agree, and Bayes just solves one route for the unknown. Example to run by hand: spam filter with P(spam)=0.2, P(word|spam)=0.6, P(word|ham)=0.1 → P(spam|word) = 0.12/0.20 = 0.6.

02Probability vs Likelihood

Intuition — the same formula, read in two directions

Take a coin with bias p. Flip it 10 times.

  • Probability: fix the parameter (p = 0.5), ask "how likely is 7 heads?" The answer is a number, and the numbers over all possible outcomes sum to 1.
  • Likelihood: fix the data (7 heads), ask "how likely is this data for each candidate p?" The answer is a curve over p. Its heights are relative — the curve doesn't sum to 1 — only the shape and the location of the peak matter.

The master mental model — a table

Imagine a table: rows are candidate parameters θ, columns are possible data outcomes. Probability reads down a column (θ fixed, data varies — the column sums to 1). Likelihood reads across a row (data fixed, θ varies — the row's heights are arbitrary; only relative height matters). StatQuest's phrasing: probability is about the data given the parameter; likelihood is about the parameter given the data.

P(X = k | θ) — probability, as a function of the data
L(θ | X = k) = P(X = k | θ) — likelihood, the same expression as a function of the parameter
Two views of the same math — drag k₀ (n = 10 flips, p = 0.5 for the probability view)
Probability — θ fixed (p=0.5), data varies. Bars over k, they sum to 1.
Likelihood — data fixed (k₀ heads), θ varies. Curve L(p)=p^k₀(1−p)^(10−k₀); only the peak matters.
Same formula, two directions. Probability predicts before you see data; likelihood infers after you've seen it. The likelihood curve here is a cross-section of the "mountain" that section 08 climbs.

When to use which

  • Probability — when you want to predict: "what will I see?" (betting, risk, queuing).
  • Likelihood — when you want to infer: "given what I saw, what's the world like?" (parameter estimation). This is the engine of MLE and, much later, of LLM training — maximizing the probability of the next token is likelihood maximization in disguise.

03The Normal (Gaussian) Distribution

Intuition — the bell

One symmetric peak at the mean μ, spread controlled by σ. Where does it come from? Add up many small independent effects and their sum (or average) becomes bell-shaped — that's the Central Limit Theorem, and it's why measurement error, sensor noise, heights, and test scores all look like this. The normal is the "many small causes" distribution.

The master mental model — center and shoulders

μ decides where the bell sits; σ decides how wide. The inflection points (where the curve stops bending down and starts bending out) sit exactly at μ ± σ — the "shoulders." And the 68-95-99.7 rule: 68% of the mass sits within one σ of the center, 95% within two, 99.7% within three.

f(x) = 1 / (σ√(2π)) · exp( −(x−μ)² / (2σ²) )
The bell — μ slides it, σ spreads it. Shaded bands = μ±1σ / 2σ / 3σ
Watch the inflection points (dots) at μ±σ — the shoulders mark one standard deviation. Narrow σ → tall, tight bell; wide σ → short, flat bell.

When to use it — and why over alternatives

  • Symmetric continuous measurements with no reason to expect skew: sensor noise, model residuals, averages of many things.
  • The default first guess for continuous data — when in doubt and the data looks symmetric, this is the honest starting point.
  • Chosen over Poisson/Exponential when values can be negative and spread both ways; over uniform when you expect a center and a spread rather than flatness.
  • Why ML loves it: the exponent makes logarithms trivial — the log-likelihood becomes a simple quadratic (squared error!). That's the hidden reason least squares = MLE under normal noise, and why Gaussians appear in weight initialization, error models, and Gaussian processes.

From our discussion

StatQuest's MLE example used the normal — not because MLE belongs to it, but because the normal is the most common family. Bonus: it has two parameters (μ and σ²), so its likelihood "mountain" lives in 2D instead of 1D (see the contour figure in section 08).

04The Binomial Distribution

Intuition — counting successes

Flip a coin n times. How many heads? That count is binomial: n fixed trials, each independent, each with the same success probability p. The shape depends on p: symmetric at p=0.5, skewed toward the rarer outcome when p is far from 0.5. The peak sits near the mean np.

The master mental model — n identical coins

You're rolling the same loaded die n times and counting how often a particular face lands. Two knobs: how many trials (n) and how loaded the coin is (p). Mean = np, variance = np(1−p). When n is small, the bars look jagged and asymmetric; when n is large, the binomial becomes the normal bell — one family, two faces.

P(X = k) = C(n, k) · p^k · (1−p)^(n−k)   ·   mean = np, var = np(1−p)
Counting successes — n trials, success probability p. Highlight = bar nearest the mean np
Crank n up to 30 with p=0.5: the bars smooth into a bell (binomial → normal). Set p low and watch the skew — rare successes bunch at small k.

When to use it — and why over alternatives

  • Fixed number of trials, independence, constant p, and you count successes: free-throw streaks, "will each of 10 users click?", defective parts in a batch of n.
  • Chosen over Poisson when you have a fixed, known n; over normal when n is small (the distribution is discrete and possibly skewed).
  • Bridge: for large n it approaches the normal; for tiny p it approaches the Poisson. The binomial is the "parent" of both.

From our discussion

Flip the formula around — fix the observed k, let p vary — and the binomial PMF is the likelihood curve L(p) = p^k(1−p)^(n−k) from sections 02 and 08. That's the two-viewpoint trick again: one formula, probability when you fix p, likelihood when you fix the data.

05The Poisson Distribution

Intuition — counting rare events

Emails arriving per hour, calls to a support line per minute, radioactive decays per second. You know the average rate λ, but each individual event is rare and independent. The Poisson counts them: one knob (λ), and the surprising fingerprint is mean = variance = λ — a distribution whose spread is exactly its average.

The master mental model — many chances, each nearly impossible

The Poisson is the limit of a binomial with huge n and tiny p: a million opportunities, each with probability λ/1,000,000 of happening. The binomial formula would explode to compute; the Poisson collapses to a single elegant expression. Small λ → skewed, bunched near 0. Large λ → smooth and bell-like (it, too, becomes the normal).

P(X = k) = λ^k e^(−λ) / k!   ·   mean = variance = λ
Counting rare events — rate λ. Highlight = mode ≈ floor(λ)
λ=0.5: most hours have zero emails. λ=15: the bars smooth toward a bell. Watch how the spread (variance) grows with λ — mean = variance, always.

When to use it — and why over alternatives

  • Counts in time or space with no natural cap: arrival counts, defect counts, particles, page hits per minute.
  • Chosen over binomial when n is unknown or huge and p is tiny — the binomial genuinely collapses to the Poisson in that limit, so why carry the extra baggage?
  • Chosen over normal when counts are small (discrete, can't be negative).

From our discussion

MLE of λ is the sample mean — the same answer as the coin (Bernoulli), reached by different calculus. The pattern that emerged in our chat: many families' MLEs end up being "some simple statistic of the data" — often the mean. The Exponential in the next section is the exception that proves the rule.

06The Exponential Distribution

Intuition — the waiting time between events

If emails arrive at rate λ per hour (Poisson), the time until the next email is exponential. The curve starts tall at t=0 — short gaps are common — and decays: long waits are rare. One knob (the rate λ), and the expected gap is 1/λ.

The master mental model — memorylessness

The Exponential is the only continuous distribution with no memory: if you've already waited s minutes with no email, the remaining wait behaves as if you had just started. The process doesn't know you've been waiting. (This is why "time to next event" is memoryless but "time since last event" isn't.)

f(t) = λ e^(−λt), t ≥ 0   ·   E[T] = 1/λ   ·   P(T > t) = e^(−λt)
Waiting time — rate λ. Shaded = the tail beyond the mean gap
The shaded tail: P(T > 1/λ) = e⁻¹ ≈ 0.368 — about 37% of gaps outlast the average gap. A bigger λ means events arrive faster, gaps shrink, the curve falls sooner.

When to use it — and why over alternatives

  • Continuous time-to-event data: battery life, service times, time-to-failure, inter-arrival times in queues.
  • Chosen over Poisson when the variable is a duration, not a count — the Poisson counts events, the Exponential times the gaps between them.
  • Chosen over normal when durations are skewed (must be ≥ 0; many short, few long).

From our discussion

The surprise from our chat: MLE of the rate is λ̂ = 1/x̄ — the reciprocal of the mean gap, not the mean itself. Same recipe as the coin (likelihood → log → differentiate → solve), different answer, because here the parameter sits in the exponent of a decay, not the base of a power. The "same procedure, different mechanics" lesson in its purest form.

07The Uniform Distribution

Intuition — total ignorance, honestly declared

Every value in a range equally likely: a fair die, a well-seeded random number generator, "pick a random point in this box." No center, no shoulders, no decay — just a flat line. In the continuous case, probability = area: intervals of equal width have equal probability, and the whole area under the line is 1.

The master mental model — the "no information" prior

When you know only the range and genuinely have no reason to prefer any value, the uniform is the honest statement of ignorance. It's also the cleanest possible place to see that probability = area under a curve — the exact connection to integration from calculus.

f(x) = 1 / (b−a), a ≤ x ≤ b   ·   mean = (a+b)/2, var = (b−a)²/12
Continuous uniform on [0, 10] — drag c and d. Probability = shaded area
Equal widths → equal probabilities. Any 2-unit-wide interval has the same 20% chance, wherever it sits. That flatness is the whole personality of the distribution.

When to use it — and why over alternatives

  • Unbiased random selection (sampling, shuffling, Monte Carlo), round-off errors, and as a placeholder prior when you know the range but nothing else.
  • Chosen over other distributions when any shape assumption would be dishonest — if you don't know the shape, pretending you do (normal) injects information you don't have.
  • Discrete uniform (the die: each face 1/6) is the same idea for a finite set of outcomes.

08Maximum Likelihood Estimation

Intuition — the mountain

You have data. The true parameter is unknown. For each candidate θ, ask: "if θ were the truth, how likely is the data I actually observed?" The answer is the height of a "mountain" over the parameter space. MLE = walk to the peak. The θ at the top is the parameter that makes your data most plausible.

The master move — it's not normal-only, and you choose the family first

StatQuest's example used the normal because it's the most famous family — but MLE works for any distribution you can write down. The catch: you must choose the family before MLE starts. Sections 03–07 are the menu; the data type dictates the choice (counts → Poisson, yes/no → Binomial, durations → Exponential, symmetric noise → Normal). MLE only tunes the parameters within the family you picked. Garbage family → confidently wrong answer.

Formal theory (brief)

Multiply the probability of each observation together — that's the likelihood. Maximizing it is hard with products, so take the log: it's monotonic, so the peak doesn't move, and products become sums. Then differentiate, set to zero, and solve. Several parameters → several equations.

L(θ) = Π f(xᵢ | θ)   →   ℓ(θ) = Σ ln f(xᵢ | θ)   →   θ̂ = argmax ℓ(θ)
Climbing the mountain — n flips, k heads. Toggle between L(p) and ln L(p): the peak doesn't move
The dashed line marks p̂ = k/n — the peak. Flip the toggle: the log curve is a different shape entirely, yet the peak sits at exactly the same p. That's monotonicity.
The normal's mountain is 2D — contour lines of the likelihood over (μ, σ²)

Two parameters → a mountain over a plane. The peak sits at μ̂ = x̄ (sample mean) and σ̂² = Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²/n (sample variance). Every ring is an equal-likelihood contour.

When to use it — and why over alternatives

  • The default principled estimator for parameters from data, for any family you've chosen. It answers the natural question: "which parameters make my observed data most plausible?"
  • Chosen over method-of-moments or guessing because it's statistically principled (and in most cases, efficient and consistent).
  • The secret identity of two things you'll meet later: least squares = MLE under normal noise (that's why the normal's log-likelihood is a quadratic), and cross-entropy loss = negative log-likelihood — which is literally what LLM training maximizes.

The family table (same recipe, different mechanics)

FamilyData it modelsParameterMLEIntuition
Bernoulli / Binomialyes/no counts in n trialspk/nshare of successes
Poissonrare-event countsλx̄average rate
Exponentialwaiting timesλ1/x̄inverse of mean gap
Normalsymmetric continuousμ, σ²x̄, Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²/ncenter & spread
Uniform"no idea" rangea, bmin(xᵢ), max(xᵢ)range endpoints

From our discussion (the full thread)

  • Not normal-only: the StatQuest example just used the most famous family. MLE is a generic procedure.
  • Choose the family BEFORE MLE: the data type dictates the family; MLE only tunes within it.
  • Same recipe every time: likelihood → log → differentiate → solve. Same procedure, different mechanics per family (see the table).
  • Why the log is legal: monotonic transformations never move a peak — the mountain's top stays the top.
  • Caveats: (1) wrong family → confident garbage; (2) MLE ≠ "probability that θ is this value" — that's the Bayesian posterior (section 01); (3) MLE can be biased — the famous σ̂² dividing by n instead of n−1 is a correction to the MLE, not the MLE itself.

09CDF & PDF — describing a random variable

Intuition — one universal description, two dialects

You've met distributions as lists of outcomes with weights (a die: six faces, 1/6 each) and as density curves (the normal bell). The formalism names them: the PMF (probability mass function — the discrete weights themselves) and the PDF (probability density function — the continuous curve). But there's a third description that works for both with a single definition, and the whole course hangs off it:

CDF: F(x) = P(X ≤ x)   —   "how much mass sits at or below x"

The CDF is a running total. Walk along the number line and keep a running tally of all the probability you've passed — that's F(x). It starts at 0 (nothing below −∞), ends at 1 (everything below +∞), and never decreases (you only ever add). Everything about a random variable — its median, its quantiles, its expectation — can be recovered from this one curve. Discrete and continuous are just two shapes of the same object: a staircase vs a smooth ramp.

Discrete = staircase · continuous = ramp · linked by the FTC

  • Discrete: a die's CDF jumps by 1/6 at each face. The jump height at x is the PMF: F(x) = Σ p(xᵢ) over all xᵢ ≤ x. The CDF is the PMF accumulated.
  • Continuous: the density is the slope of the CDF; the CDF is the area under the density. That's exactly the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus from your calculus phase, in probability clothing: F(x) = ∫₋∞^x f(t)dt and f(x) = F′(x) — CDF = antiderivative of the PDF.
  • Interval probabilities fall straight out: P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a) = ∫ₐ^b f(x)dx — one subtraction, no separate sum or integral.
One definition, three shapes — the same F(x) = P(X ≤ x), both dialects
Left: the discrete dialect — the die's CDF is a staircase; each jump's height is that face's PMF. Middle and right: the same exponential in the two continuous dialects — read the dashed x₀ = 1.2 line across both: its height on the CDF (middle, F ≈ 0.70) equals the shaded area under the PDF (right, F ≈ 0.70). That equality — CDF = antiderivative of PDF, slope of CDF = density — is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Why "density" — and the one weird consequence

  • f(x) is mass per unit length, not mass. It can exceed 1 (a tall, skinny curve) — only areas under it are probabilities. That's why f has units "1/seconds" for a time variable, and why the exponential's f(0) = λ is literally a rate (events per second, section 06).
  • P(X = x) = 0 for a continuous X — always. A single point has zero width, so zero area under the curve. "The light turns at exactly 2.000000… seconds" has probability 0 — though it can still happen (probability zero ≠ impossible; these are the measure-zero events that matter for technicalities later). What carries weight is intervals: P(1.9 < X < 2.1).
  • Median & quantiles = read the CDF backward. The median m satisfies F(m) = ½. For the exponential: 1 − e^(−λm) = ½ → m = ln2/λ ≈ 0.693/λ, below the mean 1/λ — the same right-tail story as section 10.

Gut checks

  • Uniform(0,2): write F(x) and f(x) by hand. F(x) = x/2 on [0,2], f(x) = ½ — and F′(x) = ½ = f(x) ✓.
  • Exponential(λ): confirm F(0) = 0, F(∞) = 1, and P(T > t) = 1 − F(t) = e^(−λt) — the survival probability from section 06.
  • True or false: if f(3) = 0.4, then P(X = 3) = 0.4. False — P(X = 3) = 0 for a continuous X; f is a density, not a probability.

10Expectation — the weighted average

Intuition — it's just the average, generalized

The plain average (x₁+…+xₙ)/n quietly assumes every item weighs the same: Σxᵢ·(1/n). The weighted average is Σxᵢwᵢ/Σwᵢ. Expectation = weighted average where the weights are probabilities: E[X] = Σxᵢpᵢ. The denominator Σpᵢ = 1 — probability's "sums to 1" rule — makes the divisor vanish. So the arithmetic mean is the special case where all outcomes are equally likely (pᵢ = 1/n), and expectation is the general case. "MLE equals the sample mean" (section 08) is the same idea from the data side: the long-run average of samples converges to E[X].

Two ways to feel it

1. Balance point. Lay the outcomes on a number line, put a weight of size pᵢ at each. E[X] is where the seesaw balances. Weighted coin (X=0 with p=0.25, X=1 with p=0.75) balances at 0.75 — more weight at 1. A die balances at 3.5, dead center. The distribution says how the mass is spread; the expectation says where it's centered. Probability asks "how much mass here?"; expectation asks "mass × where the mass is."

2. Long-run average. Repeat the random process many times and average the results — the sample mean converges to E[X] (Law of Large Numbers). Your numpy checks already showed this: binomial(10, 0.5) mean ≈ 5 = np; poisson(4) mean ≈ 4 = λ. Monte Carlo is expectation by simulation.

The casino example: a $5 ticket with a 1-in-a-million chance of $2,000,000 gives E[win] = 2,000,000 × 0.000001 = $2 — a $3 loss per play on average. Casinos are expectation machines: every game has negative expected value for you, and the Law of Large Numbers guarantees they collect it. Expectation is how you price risk — buffer sizing, queue delay, retransmission budgets are all expectation computations in disguise.

discrete: E[X] = Σ xᵢ·pᵢ   ·   continuous: E[X] = ∫ x·f(x)dx   ·   linearity: E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y]
The balance point — where the probability mass centers
Top: the weighted coin — the 0.75 mass at 1 pulls the balance point to 0.75. Bottom: a fair die — six equal masses balance exactly in the middle (3.5, which no single roll ever produces).

Continuous case — where your calculus lands

For a continuous variable the sum becomes an integral: E[X] = ∫x·f(x)dx. Same weighted average, but the weights are infinitesimal slices: each contributes value × density × thickness = x·f(x)dx. Picture the density curve as a shape cut from cardboard — the expectation is where it balances on a knife edge. Uniform [0,1] → 1/2, obviously the middle. Exponential → E[T] = ∫₀^∞ λt·e^(−λt)dt = 1/λ, the balance point of that lopsided curve (checklist item!). Integration and expectation are the same move: FTC gave you ∫f = F; expectation is ∫x·f — the first moment, the center-of-mass integral.

Two honest caveats — the name "expected" is a lie

  • "Expected" ≠ what happens in one trial. You never roll a 3.5; the lottery winner gets $2M, not $2. Expectation is an average over many trials, not a prediction for one. In a single trial you get a sample from the distribution; only the long-run average matches E[X].
  • Mean ≠ median for skewed distributions. The exponential has mean 1/λ but P(T > 1/λ) = e⁻¹ ≈ 0.368 — more than half the mass sits below the mean. The heavy right tail drags the balance point past the "typical" value. The mean is where the mass balances, not where most of it lives.

Expectation vs probability vs likelihood — who varies, who's fixed

AnswersVaries overUnits"Sums to" rule
Probability"How much mass on this event?"events (fixed distribution)dimensionless, [0,1]sums to 1 over events
Expectation"What's the typical value of X?"the values of X (fixed distribution)units of X — $, seconds, countnot a probability — it's a weighted mean
Likelihood"How well does θ explain the data?"parameters (fixed data)arbitrary heightnothing — only ratios and the peak matter
  • Probability and expectation read the distribution forward (distribution fixed → answer a question about it). Likelihood reads backward: data fixed, parameter varies — that's why it's a curve over θ, not a number (sections 02, 08).
  • Expectation is a summary of a distribution; probability is a question about it. A distribution is a complete description; E[X] is one number squeezed out of it (variance, median, … are more). Expectation is not "the probability of anything" — it lives in X's units.
  • Probability's answer is a chance; expectation's answer is an amount. P(X ≥ 3) = 0.5 is a chance; E[X] = 3.5 is a value. When the MLE table says "Poisson's MLE is x̄", the bridge is: the sample mean estimates E[X], and for Poisson E[X] = λ — expectation is the invisible link between "data average" and "distribution parameter."
  • Linearity — the superpower (for later): E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y]. Weighted sums distribute, so you can compute expectations of complicated things from parts without the full distribution. Entropy is E[−ln p] — an expectation of a function of X — and every loss function you'll meet in optimization is an expected loss.

The intuition that dissolves the formula: expectation is just the weighted average with probabilities as weights, the plain mean being the equal-weight special case. Two faces: the balance point of the probability mass (geometric) and the long-run sample average (Law of Large Numbers — your numpy Monte Carlo checks demonstrate it). 11Expectation of functions — the E[g(X)] machine

Intuition — one machine, every moment is a special case

Section 10's expectation was a weighted average of the values x. Nothing stops you from averaging any function of X instead — the weights stay exactly the same; only the thing being averaged changes:

E[g(X)] = Σ g(xᵢ)·p(xᵢ)   ·   E[g(X)] = ∫ g(x)·f(x)dx

The probability weights don't know or care what you're averaging — E[g(X)] is the weighted average of g's outputs, using X's weights. Mean is g(x) = x. Variance (section 12) is g(x) = (x−μ)². Second moment is g(x) = x². Entropy (info theory, next) is E[−ln p(X)]. Every loss function in ML is E[loss(X)]. It's the most reused machine in the whole course.

The trap — E[g(X)] ≠ g(E[X])

Linearity (section 10) only lets you pull linear moves through the expectation: E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b. A nonlinear g gets inside the average — the average of g's outputs is generally not g of the average input:

convex g ⟹ E[g(X)] ≥ g(E[X])   (Jensen)  ·  E[X²] ≥ (E[X])²  ·  E[1/X] ≠ 1/E[X]

Why: a convex function bows upward — the outputs at extreme inputs sit above the straight-line interpolation between them. Weighted coin (X = 1 with p = 0.75): E[X] = 0.75 but E[X²] = 0.75 ≠ 0.75² = 0.5625. The squaring happens before the averaging, so the 0.75 of mass at 1 contributes 1²·0.75 = 0.75.

Jensen in one picture — on the curve vs on the chord
The parabola is convex, so the chord between the two mass points sits above the curve. E[g(X)] — the average of the outputs — lands on the chord (amber dot); g(E[X]) — the output of the average — lands on the curve (green dot) below it. The vertical gap between them is Jensen's gap.

Worked examples — the ones that actually bite

  • Die: E[X] = 3.5; E[X²] = (1+4+9+16+25+36)/6 = 91/6 ≈ 15.17. Then Var = E[X²] − (E[X])² = 91/6 − 12.25 = 35/12 ≈ 2.92 — the die variance, produced by the same machine as the mean. This is why variance needs E[g(X)]: "mean of squares minus square of mean" (section 12) is the machine run twice.
  • Exponential — the honest shocker (from our discussion): for one exponential sample, E[1/X] = ∫₀^∞ (1/x)·λe^(−λx)dx = ∞. The weights near 0 are tiny, but 1/x blows up fast enough to give the integral infinite total mass. Jensen had already promised E[1/X] ≥ 1/E[X] = λ — the bound is satisfied, dramatically.
  • And the n−1 sequel: with n samples, E[1/x̄] = nλ/(n−1) — finite, but too big by the factor n/(n−1). The MLE 1/x̄ systematically over-estimates the rate, exactly as σ̂² under-estimates variance (section 08's caveat). Bias isn't fixed by hand-waving — it's a number you compute with this machine.

From our discussion — "doesn't the RV assign weights?"

Yes — and this machine is exactly those weights in action. The RV's induced weights (its law: P(X = x) = P({ω : X(ω) = x})) are precisely the p(xᵢ) that E[g(X)] averages over; the RV partitions the original weights into bins, it never re-weights them. The genuine traps live in functions of X: E[g(X)] ≠ g(E[X]) (Jensen, this section), non-injective transforms folding weights (lecture 10's derived distributions), and confusing which projection of a joint you're holding (sections 13–16). All accepted traits — the distribution is the summary; the discipline is knowing which summary you're holding.

Gut checks

  • Weighted coin (X = 1 with p = 0.75): confirm E[X²] = 0.75 and (E[X])² = 0.5625. Which is bigger, and why? E[X²] — squaring happens before averaging.
  • X ~ Uniform(0,1): E[X²] = ∫₀¹x²dx = 1/3 vs (E[X])² = 1/4. The Jensen gap is 1/12 — which is exactly Var(X) = (b−a)²/12 from section 12. The gap between E[X²] and (E[X])² IS the variance.
  • Linearity still applies inside: E[(X+Y)²] = E[X²] + 2E[XY] + E[Y²] — the sum distributes, but you still need the joint E[XY] (sections 13–16).

12Variance — the second moment

Intuition — how spread out the mass is

Expectation answered "where does the mass sit?". Variance answers "how spread out is it around there?". Same weighted-average machinery as E[X], one new what: instead of averaging x, average the squared distance from the mean:

Var(X) = Σ (xᵢ − μ)²·pᵢ   ·   continuous: Var(X) = ∫(x−μ)²·f(x)dx   ·   σ = √Var(X)

Why square the distances? (three reasons)

  • Plain distances cancel to zero — always. Σ(xᵢ−μ)pᵢ = 0 by construction: that's what "balance point" means, deviations on both sides offset. So you must make every distance positive before summing — squaring is the standard way. This is the deep reason variance exists: the mean is defined so deviations sum to zero, making "average deviation" useless.
  • Squaring punishes outliers harder. A deviation of 2 counts 4× more than a deviation of 1 — the same philosophy as least squares in the Linear Algebra phase: measure error by squared distance.
  • It's smooth. |x−μ| has a kink at μ; (x−μ)² is differentiable everywhere. Optimization (gradient descent, later) needs smooth.
Same center, different spread — the entire point of variance
Both distributions have mean 2 — the balance points coincide. Variance is the only thing that separates them: squared distance from the mean, weighted by probability (row 2: two beads each ½ away by 2 → Var = 4).

Three things to nail down

  • σ for intuition, Var for math. Variance lives in X² (dollars² — meaningless units); σ = √Var is in X's units — the "typical distance from the mean."
  • Variance is quadratic, not linear. E[aX+b] = aE[X]+b but Var(aX+b) = a²Var(X). Shifting changes nothing (spread doesn't care where you stand); doubling all values quadruples variance (distances double, squares ×4). Double your bet → average ×2, risk ×4.
  • The one algebra trick worth doing by hand (pure algebra — linearity from section 10): Var = E[(X−μ)²] = E[X²] − 2μE[X] + μ² = E[X²] − μ² — "mean of squares minus square of mean." Worked example (weighted coin, section 10): E[X]=0.75, E[X²]=0.25·0+0.75·1=0.75 → Var = 0.75−0.75² = 0.1875, σ ≈ 0.43.

Family fingerprints (sections 03–07)

FamilyE[X]Var(X)Fingerprint

Your checklist's numpy checks are variance checks: np.random.binomial(10, 0.5, 100000).var() ≈ 2.5 = np(1−p) and np.random.poisson(4, 100000).var() ≈ 4 = λ. Sample variance converges to Var(X) exactly like sample mean converges to E[X] — Law of Large Numbers, second moment edition.

Where it sits — and why your embedded track owns it

Extending the section 10 table: probability = a chance; expectation = an amount (first moment — location); variance = a spread (second central moment). Both expectation and variance are summaries of a distribution — squeezed-out numbers where the distribution is the full description. The pair (μ, σ) summarizes a distribution crudely but powerfully — for the Gaussian it is the distribution. The E[g(X)] machine (section 11) is one machine producing both: mean (g = x) and spread (g = (x−μ)²). Every Kalman filter carries a mean and a covariance: mean = "where you think you are", covariance = "how sure you are". Sensor noise, ADC jitter, packet jitter — "noise" is variance. When you hit the EKF (Taylor linearization + Gaussian tracking), this is the vocabulary.

From our discussion

No new concept beyond expectation — variance is the same weighted-average machine with (x−μ)² as the thing averaged. The zero-cancellation insight is the keeper: because the mean is defined so plain deviations sum to zero, you must square to get any nonzero spread measure. Physics tie-in: the second central moment is literally the moment of inertia of the probability mass about its balance point — spread the beads, harder to spin. Gut checks: (1) X = 2 surely vs Y = 0 or 4 with p=½ — both E=2, Var 0 vs 4; (2) weighted coin → 0.1875 via E[X²]−μ²; (3) np.random.normal(0,1,100000).var() ≈ 1.

13Joint densities — two random variables at once

Intuition — the honest, complete picture

So far each random variable described one experiment alone. Real questions involve pairs — packet size and latency, temperature and battery, token and position. The joint distribution is the complete picture: the weights assigned to pairs of outcomes, keeping the coupling between X and Y.

joint PMF: p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y)   ·   joint PDF: f(x, y) — mass per unit area
  • Discrete = a table. Two dice: 36 cells, each with weight 1/36. The joint PMF is a table of weights over (x, y) pairs; the weights over all cells sum to 1.
  • Continuous = a surface. f(x, y) is mass per unit area over the (x, y)-plane; probabilities are volumes under the surface: P((X, Y) ∈ R) = ∫∫_R f(x, y) dx dy, and the total volume under the surface is 1.
  • Independence = factorization. X and Y are independent iff p(x, y) = p(x)·p(y) (or f(x, y) = f(x)·f(y)): every cell is the product of its row and column totals. Test any table by multiplying the marginals (section 14) and comparing. Independence means knowing X tells you nothing about Y — the joint is fully determined by the two single-variable distributions.
The unit square — the simplest joint: X, Y independent uniforms on [0,1]
Independent uniforms: the joint density is a flat slab of height 1, so probabilities are areas. X+Y ≤ 1 is the lower-left triangle — area ½. When the surface is not flat, areas become volumes (integrals) and the game gets real — that's the workhorse f(x,y) = x+y next.

The workhorse — f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square

Check first that it's a valid density: ∫₀¹∫₀¹ (x+y) dx dy = 1 (total volume 1 ✓). It does not factorize into g(x)·h(y), so the pair is dependent — knowing Y changes X's distribution. But here's the subtle part: the surface is tallest at the top-right corner (1,1), yet the conditional mean of X actually decreases as Y grows (section 16 computes it: 2/3 → 5/9). "Tallest corner" ≠ "positively correlated" — the mass also spreads along the top edge and the right edge, and the dependence direction is only visible through the conditional/marginal machinery (sections 14–16). Keep this example: it's the workhorse for everything that follows.

14Marginal densities — pooling the other variable away

Intuition — the joint table's column totals

The joint is the full picture, but often you only care about X — "who cares what Y did; pool over all of it." The marginal is exactly that: the single-variable distribution you get by summing (discrete) or integrating (continuous) over every possible Y.

discrete: p(x) = Σ_y p(x, y)   ·   continuous: f(x) = ∫ f(x, y) dy

Read it straight off a table: the marginal of X is the column totals; the marginal of Y is the row totals. In the two-dice table (36 cells of 1/36), each column sums to 6/36 = 1/6 — X alone is uniform, as it must be. The marginal has forgotten Y completely — it's the projection of the joint onto the X-axis. (This is the information loss you asked about: the joint knows the pairing, the marginal doesn't — exactly like a 2D point's x-coordinate knows nothing about its y.)

Marginalizing the workhorse — f(x,y) = x+y → f(x) = x + ½
Left: the joint surface with columns outlined — the marginal sums the whole column at each x. Right: the result, f(x) = x + ½, a straight ramp. X is not uniform even though the domain is a square: the y-dependence leaks into the x-marginal as a slope.

The trap — a slice is not a marginal

Fixing y and looking at f(x, y₀) — a slice of the surface — is neither the marginal nor the conditional. The slice has the right shape in x but the wrong normalization (its area over x is not 1). The marginal integrates over all y; the conditional (section 15) normalizes the slice to total 1. Three objects, three questions: "ignoring Y" (marginal), "given Y = y" (conditional), "the joint itself" (slice).

Not everything is a marginal. The sum S = X + Y of two dice is a derived distribution — a function of the pair, not a projection — and its PMF is the triangle 1/36, 2/36, …, 6/36, …, 1/36 (convolution; lecture 12). Marginals pool one variable away; derived distributions transform the pair into something new. Same instinct as section 11: functions of random variables are their own topic.

15Conditional density — the slice, renormalized

Intuition — conditioning is the same move as section 01

Bayes' theorem started as "given B, zoom into the B-chunk and reweight." Nothing changes when the "given" is a value of a random variable instead of an event: you zoom into the row (or column) where Y = y and renormalize so the weights in that row sum to 1.

discrete: p(x | y) = p(x, y) / p(y)   ·   continuous: f(x | y) = f(x, y) / f(y)

The joint table again: the conditional of X given Y = y is the row y, divided by the row total. Division by p(y) is just renormalization — "within everyone with Y = y, what share has X = x?". Same for densities: the conditional density is the slice of the surface at height y, rescaled so the area under it is 1.

The shape is the slice — normalization is the only change

For a fixed y, p(y) (or f(y)) is a constant — it doesn't depend on x. So the shape of f(x | y) as a function of x is exactly the shape of the slice f(x, y); dividing by f(y) only sets the total area to 1. Contrast with the marginal (section 14), which pooled all y: marginal = "forget Y", conditional = "pretend you know Y exactly".

Workhorse slices — f(x,y) = x+y, three "givens"
Same joint, three givens. Given y=0, X is pushed hard toward 1 (density 2x — twice as much mass at x=1 as at x=0). Given y=1 the density is taller but flatter — the relative preference for large x weakens, and X's conditional mean drops from 2/3 to 5/9 (section 16). Middle panel (y=½) coincides with the marginal f(x) = x+½ — a coincidence of this example (f(½) = 1), not a rule.

Bayes on densities — the mammogram tree becomes an integral

The section 01 machinery carries over with sums becoming integrals:

f(x | y) = f(y | x)·f(x) / ∫ f(y | x′)·f(x′) dx′

The numerator is the joint f(x, y) written two ways (chain rule: f(x,y) = f(x|y)·f(y) = f(y|x)·f(x)); the denominator is the marginal f(y) — "total probability", integrated. The mammogram's 99/198 tree is the discrete special case; the integral is what you do when the "given" is continuous — sensor readings, embeddings, packet delays.

16Conditional expectation — the group means, averaged

Intuition — split, average within, then average the averages

You know E[X] — the balance point of X's distribution. Conditional expectation asks: knowing Y = y, where does X balance now? Same expectation machine (section 10), but the weights are the conditional distribution (section 15):

E[X | Y = y] = Σ x·p(x|y)   ·   E[X | Y = y] = ∫ x·f(x|y) dx

For each fixed y this is a plain number — the mean of X within the Y = y group. But y itself is random, so as y varies you get a function of y — and composing it with the random Y makes E[X | Y] a random variable. This is the big conceptual step: one expression, two personalities — a number when y is given, a random variable when it isn't.

The tower property — E[E[X | Y]] = E[X]

The group means, averaged over the groups (weighted by group size), give back the overall mean — a weighted average of weighted averages, the same machine run twice. And it's not a curiosity: E[X | Y] is the best predictor of X from Y — the function g(Y) that minimizes E[(X − g(Y))²] is exactly the conditional expectation. This is where linear regression comes from, and what a neural net trained on squared error approximates.

Two dice: X = first die, S = X + Y. Given X, S's conditional mean is X + 3.5
Given X = 3, S = 3 + Y ranges 4..9 uniformly, so its mean is 6.5 — the group mean. The group means ride along X at slope 1. Averaging them over X's distribution recovers E[S] = 7 — the dashed line — the tower property in action.

Workhorse, continued — the x+y surface

With f(x, y) = x + y on the unit square, f(x|y) = (x+y)/(y+½) (section 15), so E[X | Y=y] = ∫₀¹ x·(x+y)/(y+½) dx = (1/3 + y/2)/(y + ½):

  • y = 0: (1/3)/(½) = 2/3 · y = 1: (1/3 + ½)/(3/2) = (5/6)/(3/2) = 5/9 ≈ 0.556 · y = ½: (1/3 + ¼)/1 = 7/12 = E[X] (the coincidence from section 15).
  • The dependence direction: knowing Y is large makes X smaller on average. The conditional density f(x|y) ∝ x + y flattens as y grows, shifting weight off the extreme x = 1 toward moderate x. This is the subtlety from section 13: the surface peaks at (1,1), yet X and Y are (slightly) negatively associated — the "tallest corner" intuition is wrong.
  • Tower by hand: E[E[X|Y]] = ∫₀¹ (1/3 + y/2)/(y+½) · (y+½) dy = ∫₀¹(1/3 + y/2)dy = 7/12 = E[X] — the (y+½) cancels, the machinery closes the loop.

Why this is the lecture that matters for LLMs

  • Next-token prediction is conditional. The model estimates P(token | context) — a conditional distribution (section 15's machinery). Perplexity (info theory, next) is built from the conditional expectation of −ln P(token | context).
  • Regression IS conditional expectation. The best predictor of Y given X in the mean-squared sense is E[Y | X] — whether the predictor is linear regression or a deep net. The tower property is why "predict the conditional mean" is the right default for squared error.
  • Embedded tie: the Kalman filter's measurement update is E[state | measurements] — a conditional expectation — made tractable by Gaussians. Same object you'll meet in the EKF.

Gut checks

  • Two dice, S = X + Y: E[S | X=1] = 4.5, E[S | X=6] = 9.5; tower: E[E[S|X]] = E[X + 3.5] = 7 ✓.
  • Workhorse: verify E[X|Y=0] = 2/3 and E[X|Y=1] = 5/9 by hand, then the tower integral above — the (y+½) cancels.
  • Sanity: if X and Y are independent, E[X | Y] = E[X] — knowing Y changes nothing, because the conditional distribution equals the marginal.
