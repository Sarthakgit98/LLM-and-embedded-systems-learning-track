01The problem — pick θ to minimize the loss

Every machine-learning task reduces to one move: choose parameters θ so that a loss function L(θ) is as small as possible. The loss measures how wrong the model is:

  • Regression: L = average squared error between predictions and truth — the (y − ŷ)² machine you know from variance (Probability section 12).
  • Classification / LLMs: L = cross-entropy H(p,q) — "how many extra bits your model pays for believing q when reality is p" (Info Theory sections 04–05).
  • Both are expectations: L(θ) = E[ℓ(x, y; θ)] — the average cost over all the data, using the Probability section 11 machine. Data in, cost out.

The loop that never changes

  1. Forward: feed data through the model → predictions.
  2. Loss: compare predictions to truth → one number.
  3. Gradient: compute how the loss changes with each parameter — the slope of L at θ.
  4. Step: nudge θ downhill, against the gradient. Repeat ①–④ thousands of times.

θ is a vector with thousands to millions of entries, so the "landscape" of L lives in a high-dimensional space — but the mechanics are exactly the 1D story of section 02. Nothing else about training is more fundamental than this loop.

02Gradient descent — linearize, step downhill

The 1D recipe first

Take f(x) = x². At any x, the derivative f′(x) = 2x tells you which way f increases. To decrease f, step against the derivative:

x ← x − η·f′(x)   ·   θ ← θ − η·∇L(θ)

Why this is Taylor in disguise (Calculus section 15): for a small step Δ, f(x+Δ) ≈ f(x) + f′(x)·Δ — the loss is locally a straight line with slope f′(x). If f′(x) > 0, f rises to the right, so step left (Δ = −η·f′(x)); if f′(x) < 0, step right. η (the learning rate) is how far you trust that local linearization. Too big and the approximation breaks (you overshoot past the minimum); too small and you crawl.

Gradient descent on a bowl — slide η and watch the steps
40 steps on f(x) = x² (minimum at x = 0). η = 0.1 crawls steadily; η = 0.5 lands almost exactly on the minimum in one step; η = 1.0 flips sign forever (x ← −x, the update is exact); η > 1 diverges. The learning rate is the single most important number you'll tune tomorrow.

Multiple dimensions — the gradient vector

With thousands of parameters, the derivative becomes the gradient ∇L — a vector whose entries are the partial derivatives ∂L/∂θᵢ. It points uphill, in the direction of steepest increase (perpendicular to the level curves — the same level-lines picture from Linear Algebra's duality section). Stepping against it, θ ← θ − η·∇L, is the steepest-descent move. That single line — with the gradient supplied by backprop — is the entire training loop.

03Landscapes aren't bowls — local minima, and where you start

Real losses are bumpy: many valleys, some shallow, some deep. Gradient descent walks into whichever valley it started in — unless the learning rate is big enough to leap a ridge.

A bumpy landscape — start position and η decide which valley you land in
Two valleys joined by a ridge: the global min at x ≈ −1.5 (f ≈ −3.2, green tick) and a shallow local min at x ≈ +1.2 (f ≈ 0.4), separated by a ridge crest at f ≈ 2.3. Start at +1.5 with η = 0.1: trapped in the local min. Start at −1.5: straight into the global min. Crank η to ~0.8 from +1.5: the steps leap the ridge and roll into the global valley. (Start exactly at a minimum and no η moves you — the gradient is zero there.) Where you start and how big your steps are decide what you find.

Honest caveat — don't over-fear minima in deep learning

  • High-dimensional losses have many local minima, but they're mostly "good enough" — in overparameterized networks, training loss often reaches near zero anyway (the interpolation regime), and the generalization gap is the real concern, not the valley you landed in.
  • Saddle points dominate in high dimensions — points where the slope is ~0 but it's not a minimum. They're more common than true minima and trickier to escape (which is part of why momentum and Adam, section 05, help).
  • Initialization matters — that's why networks start with small random weights, not zeros or big values. You'll meet "how to init" as a real design choice later; for micrograd it's just "small random numbers."

04GD vs SGD — the gradient is an expectation

The loss is L(θ) = E[ℓ(X; θ)] — an average over data. Its gradient is also an expectation:

∇L(θ) = E[∇ℓ(X; θ)]   — gradient passes through expectation (linearity, Prob §10)

This one fact splits training into two flavors:

Full-batch GDSGD (stochastic)
Gradient estimateaverage over all data — exactaverage over one sample or a small random batch — noisy
Cost per stepexpensive (all data)cheap (one batch)
Directiontrue downhillroughly downhill, wobbles
Usessmall problems, math proofseverything real — including LLM training

Why the noise is fine — and even useful

  • Law of Large Numbers (Probability §09): a batch average of gradients converges to the true expectation as batch size grows — your numpy checks (binomial mean ≈ np) are the same move: sample, average, estimate. Training just iterates it.
  • Noise is a feature: the wobble helps wander out of shallow valleys and flat spots a clean full-batch walk would stall in.
  • Minibatch is the sweet spot: one sample = too noisy; all data = too slow. A few dozen to a few hundred samples per step is the default everywhere. "Batch size" is one of the dials you'll see in every training run.

05Adam — names only (the upgrade you don't need tomorrow)

Plain GD uses one global step size η for every parameter. Adam — adaptive moment estimation — makes the step smarter per parameter, with three ideas:

  • Momentum (velocity): keep a running average of past gradients, like a ball with inertia. Zig-zag directions cancel out; consistent downhill direction accumulates speed. Escapes flat spots faster.
  • Per-parameter step sizes (RMSProp idea): track the average squared gradient of each parameter. Parameters with big past gradients get smaller steps (they're noisy/large); parameters with tiny gradients get bigger steps (they're stuck on a plateau). No more one-size-fits-all η.
  • Bias correction: the running averages start at zero, so the first few steps are corrected to avoid a cold-start bias. A detail that matters in the first iterations only.

Result: one default η (0.001-ish) that "just works" across problems — which is why Adam is the default optimizer in virtually every framework. For micrograd tomorrow, plain SGD is enough — Adam is the upgrade you'll reach for when you start tuning real networks.

06The bridge to micrograd — what tomorrow's 100 lines do

micrograd (Karpathy) is the minimal honest implementation of the loop above:

  1. Forward: build a tiny computation graph — every number is a node that remembers what made it (x, w·x, +, tanh, …). This is the chain-rule pipeline from Calculus section 06, as data.
  2. Loss: mean squared error, L = (1/n)Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² — the E[g(X)] machine with g = squared error; the same (x−μ)² as variance (Probability §12), the same least-squares-equals-MLE story (Probability §08).
  3. Backward (the star): backward() walks the graph from the loss back to the inputs, multiplying derivatives along each edge — the chain rule in ~20 lines. Every weight learns its gradient ∂L/∂wᵢ.
  4. Step: w ← w − η·∇w for every weight — section 02's line, executed on real data until the loss flattens.

Readiness check — can you say this out loud? (the Day-4 booster)

"Linearize the loss, step downhill." Gradient descent = trust the local straight-line approximation (Taylor), move against the slope (−∇L), scaled by a learning rate that respects how far the linearization is valid. SGD = estimate that slope from a random batch instead of all data. Adam = per-parameter step sizes plus momentum. If you can expand each sentence, you're ready to write the code.

Gut checks (2 minutes, by hand)

  • f(x) = x², x = 3, η = 0.1: where's the next step? x = 3 − 0.1·6 = 2.4.
  • Why does η = 1.0 on x² oscillate forever? The update is x ← x − 2x = −x — an exact flip, never approaching 0.
  • Which is noisier, GD or SGD? Which is cheaper per step? SGD on both counts — that's the trade.
  • Why does gradient pass through expectation? Both are linear in the data; linearity (Probability §10) lets you move ∇ inside E.
