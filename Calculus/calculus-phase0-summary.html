01The Big Picture

Calculus answers two questions about a function — one local, one global:

  • The derivative: "how fast is this changing right now?" → the slope of the tangent line at a point.
  • The integral: "how much has accumulated overall?" → the area under the curve.

Surprisingly, these two questions are inverses of each other — that's the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and it's the arc of the whole series. For now, the derivative side:

The master idea — local linearity: zoom in close enough on any smooth function and it looks like a straight line. The derivative is the slope of that line. Everything else — all the formulas, the product and chain rules, backprop — is just bookkeeping built on this one fact.

The habit to build: when you see df/dx, read it as "for a tiny step dx, the function rises by df ≈ (slope)·dx". The whole course is learning to compute that slope for every kind of function.

02The Paradox of the Derivative

Secant → tangent

Pick two points on a curve and connect them: that's a secant line, slope = rise/run. Now slide the second point toward the first. The secant slopes approach a limiting value — the slope of the tangent line. That limit is the derivative.

f'(x) = limh→0 (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h
From secant to tangent — drag the slider (curve: y = (x−160)²/200 + 30, at x = 100)

As Δx → 0 the secant slope converges to the tangent slope. The derivative is the limit, not any single secant.

The paradox: dx is treated as zero (we want the instantaneous rate) and simultaneously as nonzero (a slope needs a run). Resolution: df and dx are not actual quantities — the derivative is a limit of ratios, not a literal quotient. "Ghosts of departed quantities."

03Derivative Formulas Through Geometry

Three formulas, zero algebra — each is "increase the input by a tiny amount and watch where the new area/volume appears."

d/dx(x²) = 2x — the square

The square: area x² grows by two strips
Grow the side by dx: the area gains two strips, each x·dx. So d(x²) = 2x·dx, and d/dx(x²) = 2x. The corner dx² is two infinitesimals multiplied — it vanishes next to the strips.

d/dx(x³) = 3x² — the cube

Same story in 3D: grow the cube's edge by dx and you add three faces, each of area x² (the three edge-corners vanish). Hence d/dx(x³) = 3x². The pattern d/dx(xⁿ) = n·xⁿ⁻¹ is "n faces of size xⁿ⁻¹" — the power rule is just geometry in n dimensions.

d/dx(1/x) = −1/x² — the constant-area rectangle

The rectangle with area 1: width x × height 1/x
Area must stay 1 (the product is constant). Widening by dx adds the green strip dx·(1/x); the height must drop, removing the red strip x·|d(1/x)|. Equal areas ⟹ dx·(1/x) = x·|d(1/x)| ⟹ d(1/x)/dx = −1/x². The minus sign: height shrinks as x grows.

d/dx(√x) = 1/(2√x) — the square, inverted

Same picture as x², run in reverse: area x, side √x
Now x is the area and √x is the side. Adding a rim of area dx = two strips of √x·d(√x) gives dx = 2√x·d(√x) ⟹ d(√x)/dx = 1/(2√x). This is the x² proof run backwards — the geometric seed of the inverse-function idea: the derivative of an inverse is the reciprocal of the original's slope.

The teaser: d/dx(sin x) = cos x — the unit circle

The rotating stick: θ is the angle, the tip traces the circle
Rotate the stick by a tiny dθ: the tip moves dθ units along the arc (radius 1, radians), tangent to the circle. That motion decomposes into a vertical piece cos θ·dθ (sin grows) and a horizontal piece −sin θ·dθ (cos shrinks). Hence d/dθ(sin θ) = cos θ and d/dθ(cos θ) = −sin θ — each function's rate of change is the other coordinate, with a minus on cos because the circle turns counterclockwise.

04The Product Rule

(f·g)' = f'·g + f·g'

The picture is a rectangle with side lengths f and g — the product is an area. Grow both sides a little and the area grows in two strips:

(f+df)(g+dg) = fg + df·g + f·dg + df·dg
Two strips survive: df·g (right) and f·dg (top). The corner df·dg is two infinitesimals multiplied — infinitely smaller than the strips (O(dx²) vs O(dx)) — so it vanishes. Result: d(f·g) = df·g + f·dg ⟹ (fg)' = f'g + fg'.
Why the corner really vanishes (the paradox, again): it's not that df·dg is literally zero — it's that the strips are ~dx big while the corner is ~dx² big. As dx → 0, the corner's relative contribution → 0. The product rule keeps exactly the linear part of the change; everything quadratic or higher is discarded.

05The Chain Rule

d/dx f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) · g'(x)

Composition = two machines in a row. The first machine's output is the second machine's input:

df = f'(g(x)) · dg = f'(g(x)) · g'(x) · dx
A step dx enters machine g and comes out scaled by g'(x): dg = g'(x)·dx. That dg enters machine f and comes out scaled by f' evaluated at the inner value: df = f'(g(x))·dg. Total sensitivity = product of the two slopes. In Leibniz notation df/dx = df/dg · dg/dx it even looks like fraction cancellation — and because derivatives are local linear slopes, it literally is, locally.
The slope triangles — machine 1's rise becomes machine 2's run
The first triangle has run dx and rise dg (slope g'). That same dg is the run of the second triangle, whose rise is df (slope f'). Two slopes end-to-end multiply: df = f'(u)·g'(x)·dx.
Two common slips:
  1. Evaluate f' at g(x), not at x. The outer machine doesn't know x; it only sees g(x). This is the #1 sign error in practice.
  2. The chain rule is the "nested zoom." You zoom into the outer function (a sine wave, say) until it looks linear — but the input axis of that zoom is measured in units of g, and changes in g are themselves caused by changes in x through g'. Both slopes multiply.

07Rules Cheat Sheet

d/dx[c] = 0

Constants don't change — a horizontal line has slope 0.

d/dx[xⁿ] = n·xⁿ⁻¹

Power rule = "n faces of size xⁿ⁻¹". Includes √x = x^½ → ½x^(−½).

d/dx[1/x] = −1/x²

The constant-area rectangle; negative because height shrinks.

d/dx[sin x] = cos x

Vertical tip speed of the rotating stick.

d/dx[cos x] = −sin x

Horizontal tip speed, sign flipped by the counterclockwise turn.

(fg)' = f'g + fg'

Product rule = two strips of the rectangle; corner df·dg vanishes.

(f∘g)' = f'(g)·g'

Chain rule = machines in series; slopes multiply; evaluate f' at g(x).

Local linearity

The reason all of this works: zoomed in, every smooth function is its tangent line, so rates multiply and add like slopes do.

08Why This Matters — and What's Next

The chain rule is backpropagation. A neural network is one giant composition of functions (layers, activations, loss). The gradient of the loss w.r.t. any weight is a product of derivatives along the path from that weight to the loss — the chain rule, applied backward through the computation graph. Karpathy's micrograd is literally just these two rules (product + chain) plus a data structure to walk the graph. Everything you just visualized in this page is the engine of deep learning.

09Euler's Number e — The Function That Is Its Own Derivative

The question that births e

Look at exponential functions f(x) = aˣ. Their derivative is proportional to the function itself: d/dx(aˣ) = c(a)·aˣ — the bigger the value, the faster it grows. That's what "exponential" means: growth rate ∝ current size (bacteria, money, a rumor). The constant c(a) depends on the base a, and it's exactly ln(a).

d/dx(aˣ) = ln(a)·aˣ   ·   d/dx(eˣ) = eˣ   ·   d/dx(ln x) = 1/x

e is the base where the proportionality constant becomes 1 — the unique base whose function is its own derivative. Every other exponential is eˣ scaled: aˣ = e^(x·ln a). And ln(x) is the inverse function: "how long to reach x at continuous growth rate 1?"

The aˣ family — all pass through (0,1); tangent slopes there are ln(a)
eˣ is the one curve whose tangent at (0,1) has slope exactly 1 — "growing at the same rate as its own size." The little triangle marks ln(e) = 1: rise 1 over run 1.

Two faces of e

  • Derivative view: the base where d/dx(aˣ) = aˣ with no extra factor.
  • Growth view: e = lim(1+1/n)ⁿ ≈ 2.71828 — the "natural" limit of compounding: one unit of growth, chopped into infinitely many compounding steps. (Rule of 72: doubling time ≈ 72/(rate%) — because ln 2 ≈ 0.693.)
Why the derivative of ln x is 1/x: since eˣ and ln x are inverses, their slopes are reciprocals: if y = ln x then x = e^y, dx/dy = e^y = x, so dy/dx = 1/x. The chain-rule "machine reversal" from video 3, applied to the exponential machine.

10Implicit Differentiation — Slopes of Curves That Aren't Functions

The problem: x² + y² = 25 is a circle — for most x there are TWO y values, so it can't be written as y = f(x). Yet the tangent slope is well-defined everywhere. Trick: differentiate both sides, treating y as an unknown function of x — the chain rule does the rest.

x² + y² = 25  ⟹  2x + 2y·(dy/dx) = 0  ⟹  dy/dx = −x/y

The chain rule sneaks in via d/dx(y²) = 2y·y' — we never need to write y explicitly; we just acknowledge it's a function of x.

Circle x² + y² = 25 — tangent ⟂ radius, captured by the formula
At (3,4): dy/dx = −3/4. The tangent direction (−4,3) is perpendicular to the radius (3,4) — the formula encodes "tangent ⟂ radius" without any geometry. Same x = 3 gives y = ±4 (two points, two slopes ±3/4) — the ± is exactly why this curve can't be a function.

When you'd use it

  • Any curve defined by an equation (circles, ellipses x²/a² + y²/b² = 1, implicit surfaces).
  • Related rates: if two quantities are linked by an equation, differentiating both sides links their rates of change.
  • General pattern: for F(x,y) = 0, dy/dx = −(∂F/∂x)/(∂F/∂y) (implicit function theorem — the "∂" version shows up later in gradient-land).

11Limits, L'Hôpital's Rule & ε-δ Definitions

Why limits exist at all

The derivative is a limit of a 0/0 expression — (f(x+h)−f(x))/h is literally 0/0 at h = 0. Limits are the machinery that makes "what does it approach?" rigorous, and L'Hôpital's rule is the workhorse for computing such limits.

L'Hôpital, intuitively: when both f and g → 0 at a point, zoom in — each function is its tangent line through the point. If f(x) ≈ f'(0)·x and g(x) ≈ g'(0)·x, then f/g ≈ f'(0)/g'(0). The ratio of the functions is, near the point, the ratio of their local slopes.
lim f(x)/g(x) = lim f'(x)/g'(x)  (when both → 0 or both → ±∞)
Classic: lim sin(x)/x = 1. sin(x) starts out with slope cos(0) = 1, so near 0 the sine curve is indistinguishable from y = x; their ratio → 1. Likewise (eˣ − 1)/x → e⁰ = 1.

The ε-δ definition — limits without hand-waving

limx→a f(x) = L  ⟺  ∀ε>0 ∃δ>0 : |x−a|<δ ⟹ |f(x)−L|<ε

ε is the output tolerance you demand; δ is the input distance you must stay within to guarantee it. "You can get the output arbitrarily close to L — you just need to get close enough to a." No motion, no "infinitely small" — pure logical quantification.

ε-δ for f(x) = x² at a = 1 (L = 1)
The curve stays inside the horizontal ε band (yellow) for the whole vertical δ band (green). Here δ ≈ 0.18 was chosen so x² exits the ε band exactly at the δ lines — for any smaller ε you can always find a smaller δ. The limit is a guarantee structure, not a process.

12Integration & the Fundamental Theorem

Integration = accumulation

The integral adds up infinitely many tiny contributions: ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = the area under f from a to b = total accumulated quantity. It is the "undo" of the derivative — and the FTC is the exact statement of that inverse relationship.

A(x) = ∫ax f(t)dt  ⟹  A'(x) = f(x)   ·   ∫ab f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a)  (F′ = f)
  • Part 1: the derivative of the area function is the original function — adding a strip of height f(x)·dx changes the area by f(x)·dx, so the area accumulates at rate f(x).
  • Part 2: to compute an integral, find any antiderivative F and evaluate at the endpoints. This is the leap: area computed by anti-differentiation, not by summing strips.
∫₀ˣ sin(t)dt — the shaded area up to a moving point, plus the accumulating strip
Here A(x) = 1 − cos(x): as x moves, A accumulates; a tiny step adds the thin strip of height sin(x). Strip area ÷ strip width → sin(x): A' = f. This is the FTC in one glance.
Worked: ∫₀^π sin(x)dx = [−cos x]₀^π = −cos(π)+cos(0) = 2. And the average value of sin on [0,π] = 2/π — the constant height with the same area — the idea from our earlier discussion: average value of f = (F(b)−F(a))/(b−a) = average slope of the antiderivative.

14Higher-Order Derivatives

Derivatives of derivatives

The derivative tells you how fast something changes; the second derivative tells you how fast that changes — i.e., how the slope itself is tilting.

  • Position s(t) → velocity s′ → acceleration s″ → jerk s‴ (and beyond).
  • Graph reading: f″ > 0 ⟹ convex ("smiling" — slope increasing); f″ < 0 ⟹ concave ("frowning"); f″ = 0 with a sign change = inflection point (the curve flattens out mid-turn).
  • For a circle of radius r, curvature = 1/r — the second-derivative-ish measure of "how sharply the direction is changing."
f(x) = (x³ − 3x)/2 — convex right, concave left, inflection at 0
Left of 0 the slope is decreasing (concave); right of 0 the slope is increasing (convex); exactly at 0 the slope is momentarily −1.5 and stops decreasing — the inflection point.
Notation: f″ = d²y/dx² — literally "the derivative of dy/dx"; the d² and dx² are bookkeeping for "applied twice." In ML you'll meet the generalization: the Hessian matrix of all second partial derivatives — the curvature of the loss landscape, used by second-order optimizers.

15Taylor Series — Local Linearity, Upgraded

The idea

A tangent line matches a function's value and slope at a point. A parabola can also match its curvature (f″). A cubic matches f‴ too. Taylor's series matches ALL derivatives at one point — the resulting polynomial is the best local approximation at every order, and for many functions it converges to the function itself.

f(x) = f(a) + f′(a)(x−a) + f″(a)/2!·(x−a)² + f‴(a)/3!·(x−a)³ + …

Why the n! denominators: the n-th term's nth derivative is f⁽ⁿ⁾(a) — differentiating (x−a)ⁿ n times gives n!, so the coefficient must be f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n! to match. Every term is "the piece of f's behavior that shows up at order n."

eˣ vs its Maclaurin polynomials — each degree hugs longer
P₁ = 1+x, P₂ = 1+x+x²/2, P₃ = 1+x+x²/2+x³/6 — the approximations cling to eˣ near 0 and peel away further out. Since eˣ's derivatives never stop, the full series 1+x+x²/2!+x³/3!+… converges everywhere.
The eˣ bonus: differentiate 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + … and each term becomes the previous one — the series is literally self-reproducing, which is why its sum is the self-derivative function.

Radius of convergence & divergence

Matching at a point doesn't guarantee matching everywhere. The series works within a radius of convergence R around the expansion point; outside it, terms grow and the sum diverges.
